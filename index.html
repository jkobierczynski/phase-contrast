Phase Contrast Bench

Field notes

A thin, transparent specimen doesn't absorb light — it slows it down by a fraction of a wavelength wherever it's optically denser. Written as a wave, that's exp(iφ), which for a weak object is almost exactly 1 + iφ: a strong, uniform direct term plus a faint term 90° out of phase with it, carrying all of the specimen's structure. Ordinary detectors only see intensity, |amplitude|², and a 90° phase offset barely touches that — which is exactly what panel 03 shows.

The objective's back focal plane is where this splits apart physically: the direct term converges to a ring matching the condenser annulus, while the structural term — the specimen's spatial-frequency content — spreads out across the aperture, mostly missing the ring entirely. A phase ring sitting exactly on that convergence point can retard the direct beam by a further quarter wave and dim it, without touching the light that bypassed it. Now the two terms are back in step enough to interfere, and interference does show up as intensity — panel 04. Detune the ring — its radius, or its X/Y offset, in the Phase Plate group — away from the condenser and watch panel 04 lose contrast as the two stop lining up; this is the same fault a centring telescope is used to correct on a real microscope.
